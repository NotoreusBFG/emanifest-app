## Scene 1 — Cold open: two agencies, two different labels

**[VISUAL: ManifestMate logo, then split screen — left side labeled "EPA," right side labeled "US DOT" — a manifest sits in the middle]**

**NARRATION:**
"Your site already has a label from the EPA: you're a 'generator,' because
your operations produce hazardous waste. That's about your *site* — how
much waste you make, and what that triggers under RCRA — the Resource
Conservation and Recovery Act.

But the moment you personally sign Item 15 on a manifest, a second federal
agency gives *you* — the individual, not the site — a different label. To
the US Department of Transportation, you're now a 'hazmat employee,'
because signing that box is a legal certification under DOT's rules, not
EPA's. Same document, two different agencies, two different definitions —
one for your company, one for the person holding the pen."

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT: EPA → your site is a "generator"  |  US DOT → you, the signer, are a "hazmat employee" (49 CFR 171.8)]**

**NARRATION (cont.):**
"This video, and the two after it, cover the training DOT requires of that
second label — before you sign for the first time, and again every three
years after that."

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT: 49 CFR 172.704 — Training requirements]**

*Quiz tie-in (Q5): "What DOT regulation triggers hazmat employee training for anyone who signs the manifest?" — answer given directly here: 49 CFR 172.704.*

## Scene 2 — What the manifest system is actually for

**[VISUAL: Simple animated chain — Generator icon → Transporter truck icon → Disposal facility icon, each with a signature checkmark appearing in sequence]**

**NARRATION:**
"The Uniform Hazardous Waste Manifest — EPA Form 8700-22 — is the
chain-of-custody document that tracks hazardous waste from the generator,
through each transporter, to the final designated disposal facility. RCRA
calls this 'cradle to grave' responsibility — not just tracking. As the
generator, you stay legally accountable for that waste through its entire
life, even after a transporter picks it up, which is why a manifest that
never comes back signed is your problem to chase down, not something you
can just forget about."

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT: EPA Form 8700-22]**

*Quiz tie-in (Q1): "What EPA form number is the Uniform Hazardous Waste Manifest?" — 8700-22, stated on screen and spoken.*

---

## Scene 3 — Why DOT, not just EPA

**[VISUAL: Split screen — left labeled "EPA: tracks the waste," right labeled "DOT: regulates the shipment"]**

**NARRATION:**
"Here's the part people miss: the manifest is doing two jobs at once. It's
an EPA tracking document, and — separately — it's a DOT shipping paper,
because DOT requires shipping papers for any hazardous material in
transport. Rather than making generators fill out two different forms, EPA
designed one document to satisfy both requirements. That dual role is
exactly why you have two separate, stacking training obligations: DOT's
172.704, which we're covering now, and — if you're generator staff — EPA's
40 CFR 262.17 generator training, which is a different course entirely."

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT: One form, two federal agencies]**

## Scene 4 — How often, and what happens if you're new

**[VISUAL: Calendar graphic ticking forward 3 years, then resetting]**

**NARRATION:**
"Initial training is due within 90 days of being hired into a role that
signs manifests — you can sign under direct supervision during that
90-day window. After that, it's recurrent: at least once every three
years."

*Quiz tie-in (Q6): "How often must recurrent DOT hazmat employee training be completed?" — every 3 years.*

## Scene 5 — Reading the manifest itself

**[VISUAL: A real (sample/redacted) manifest on screen, with callout boxes highlighting each region as it's mentioned]**

**NARRATION:**
"Before we get into what your signature means, let's just look at the
form. Three main blocks. First, the parties: generator,
one or more transporters in the order they'll handle the shipment, and the
designated receiving facility, each identified by name, address, and EPA ID
number. Second, the waste line items — one row per distinct waste stream,
each with a DOT proper shipping name, hazard class, UN or NA ID number,
packing group, and quantity. Third, the certification blocks — that's where
each party signs off on their part of the custody chain.

One thing that surprises people: not every line on this form has to be a
DOT-regulated hazardous material, and not every line even has to be
hazardous waste. Item 9a is a simple 'X' — mark it when a line is a DOT
hazardous material, and leave it blank when it isn't. That means the same
manifest can carry hazardous waste, DOT-regulated hazardous materials, and
ordinary non-hazardous materials side by side — say, latex paint or used
oil you're consolidating into the same shipment, to the same facility. The
X is what tells everyone downstream which lines are actually regulated as
hazardous, and which aren't."

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Parties → Waste lines → Certifications]**

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Item 9a — mark "X" only for DOT-regulated lines; unmarked lines can be non-hazardous]**

**NARRATION (cont.):**
"We're not asking you to *classify* waste in this course — figuring out
what code a waste stream gets is a separate EPA competency, and if that's
part of your job, ManifestMate's Haz Waste University covers it. This
module is just about recognizing what's in front of you when you open a
manifest."
